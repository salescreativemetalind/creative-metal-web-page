## Weld Neck vs Slip-On Flange — When to Use Each

The primary difference: A **Weld Neck (WN) flange** has a long tapered hub that is butt-welded to the pipe, providing the strongest connection and best stress distribution. A **Slip-On (SO) flange** slides over the pipe and requires two fillet welds (inside and outside). WN flanges cost more but are required for high-pressure, high-temperature, and critical service. SO flanges are cheaper and easier to install for lower-pressure applications.

## Weld Neck Flange (WN/WNRF) — Detailed

**How it connects:**
The WN flange has a protruding hub with a bore that exactly matches the connected pipe's ID. The pipe end is beveled and butt-welded to the flange hub. This creates a single, full-penetration weld joint with smooth internal bore transition.

**Why the tapered hub matters:**
The hub gradually transitions from the flange thickness to the pipe wall thickness. This taper:
- Distributes stress gradually (no sharp transition)
- Reduces stress concentration at the weld
- Provides reinforcement at the most critical point
- Allows radiographic examination of the weld

**WN Flange Specifications:**
| Standard | ASME B16.5 (up to 24"), B16.47 (26"-60") |
| Pressure Classes | 150, 300, 600, 900, 1500, 2500 |
| Face Types | Raised Face (RF), Ring Type Joint (RTJ), Flat Face (FF) |
| Materials | ASTM A182 (SS/Alloy), ASTM A105 (CS) |
| Bore | Matches pipe schedule (must specify when ordering) |

## Slip-On Flange (SO/SORF) — Detailed

**How it connects:**
The pipe slides through the SO flange bore (which is slightly larger than pipe OD). The pipe end is positioned so it's recessed ~2mm from the flange face. Then two fillet welds are applied — one at the face (inside) and one at the hub (outside).

**Why two welds:**
- Inside fillet weld: Seals the pipe-to-flange junction (prevents leakage path)
- Outside fillet weld: Provides mechanical strength for the connection
- Both welds together give approximately 2/3 the strength of a WN butt weld

**SO Flange Specifications:**
| Standard | ASME B16.5 |
| Pressure Classes | 150, 300, 600 (sometimes up to 900) |
| Face Types | Raised Face (RF), Flat Face (FF) |
| Materials | ASTM A182 (SS/Alloy), ASTM A105 (CS) |
| Bore | Standard pipe OD + clearance (~1.5-3mm larger) |

## Strength & Pressure Rating

ASME B16.5 allows both WN and SO flanges in the same pressure classes, BUT:

**ASME B31.3 restrictions on SO flanges:**
- Not recommended for severe cyclic service
- Limited use above 427°C (800°F) for carbon steel
- Client specifications often restrict SO to Class 300 or 150 only
- Some companies prohibit SO flanges for hydrocarbon service entirely

**Fatigue life comparison (per ASME):**
- Weld Neck: Fatigue life = 100% (reference)
- Slip-On: Fatigue life = approximately 66% of WN
- Threaded flange: Fatigue life = approximately 33% of WN

## When to Choose Weld Neck Flange

**WN is required/recommended for:**
- All Class 600 and above applications
- All high-temperature service (>400°C)
- Hydrocarbon process piping (industry standard)
- Lethal or toxic fluid service
- Severe cyclic service (vibration, thermal cycling)
- Where radiography of weld is specified
- Offshore and subsea applications
- Steam service at high pressure
- Cryogenic service (below -29°C)
- When client specification mandates WN

## When to Choose Slip-On Flange

**SO is acceptable for:**
- Class 150 and 300 utility service (water, air, nitrogen)
- Low-pressure, low-temperature applications
- Non-hazardous fluid service
- Where cost savings are critical and code allows
- Temporary or easily replaceable connections
- Where pipe alignment is difficult (SO provides adjustment)
- Field fabrication with limited welding setup
- Architectural and non-pressure applications

## Installation Differences

### Weld Neck Installation:
1. Cut pipe square and bevel end (37.5° bevel per ASME)
2. Align pipe to flange hub (self-aligning due to bore match)
3. Tack weld in position
4. Complete root pass (TIG/GTAW recommended)
5. Fill and cap passes (SMAW or FCAW)
6. Radiograph weld (if required)
7. Hydrotest

### Slip-On Installation:
1. Cut pipe square (no beveling needed)
2. Slide flange over pipe
3. Position pipe end ~2mm back from flange face
4. Apply inside fillet weld (pipe OD to flange face)
5. Apply outside fillet weld (pipe OD to flange hub)
6. No radiography possible on fillet welds
7. Hydrotest

**Installation time:** SO flanges are approximately 30% faster to install due to no beveling requirement and simpler welding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the difference between WNRF and SORF flanges?**
A: WNRF = Weld Neck Raised Face. SORF = Slip-On Raised Face. WN has a tapered hub that butt-welds to pipe (stronger, 30-60% more expensive). SO slides over pipe with two fillet welds (weaker but cheaper and easier to install).

**Q: Can I use slip-on flanges for steam service?**
A: For low-pressure steam (< 10 bar, < 200°C), SO flanges are acceptable in most codes. For high-pressure steam (boiler main steam, HP headers), weld neck flanges are mandatory per most client specifications and IBR requirements.

**Q: Why are weld neck flanges more expensive?**
A: WN flanges require more material (the entire hub section), more machining (boring to match pipe schedule), and more complex forging. The long hub uses significantly more steel than a flat SO flange.

**Q: What does "Raised Face" mean?**
A: RF (Raised Face) means the flange has a raised area around the bore where the gasket seats. This raised area (typically 1.6mm for Class 150/300, 6.4mm for Class 600+) concentrates bolt load onto a smaller gasket area for better sealing.
